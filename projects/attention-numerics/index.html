attention-numerics

How much error does low-precision attention add, and when does rotating the inputs make it worse?

Question

Attention on recent GPUs runs in BF16 or FP8 for speed. FP8 keeps three bits of mantissa, so kernels give each tile of queries and keys its own scale, and some also rotate the inputs first to spread outlier channels across all of them. I wanted to know how much error each of those steps adds, whether long context makes it worse, and whether rotation always helps.

Method

I wrote a NumPy emulator that rounds where a tiled attention kernel does: when inputs are converted, while products accumulate, in the running softmax, and when probabilities are stored. It is checked against an independent float64 reference, on random inputs with up to 65,536 keys and on query, key and value tensors captured from four heads of Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct reading 1,024 tokens of public-domain text. On those tensors its BF16 error matches PyTorch’s own BF16 attention on the CPU: 0.17378% against 0.17375%, the median over the four heads. Everything here is emulated on a CPU; nothing was measured on a GPU.

Result

Context length mattered little. On random inputs, error barely moved between 1,000 and 64,000 keys: about 0.38% in BF16 and 5.4% in FP8 with one scale for the whole tensor. A single outlier channel mattered more. Scaled up eightfold, it pushed single-scale FP8 error to 36%, and per-tile scales brought it back to 3.9%.

Rotation can turn a harmless component into a large error. In layer 0 of the real model, 97% and 95% of the keys’ energy sits in their mean, a vector shared by every key. On its own that component is harmless: it shifts all of a query’s scores by the same amount, and softmax cancels it. With per-tile FP8, error in those two heads was 24% and 21%. Rotating first raised it to 117% and 73%, larger than the output itself; at the worst query rows nearly all of the attention weight moved to different keys. Rotation mixes the shared component into every channel, where rounding no longer treats all keys alike. In layer 12, where no such component dominates, rotation helped a little.

Matched synthetic controls separate the two cases. With one query and key channel set to 32 for every token, rotation raised error from 4.7% to 41%. With the same channel multiplied by 32 instead, so that it still varied from token to token, rotation cut error from 43% to 4.3%. FP8 already stores an exponent for every value, so spreading an outlier helps less predictably than it does on an integer grid.

Subtracting the mean key before quantizing, as SageAttention does, cut the layer-0 errors to 5.5% and 12% without rotation, and to 45% and 27% with it. That repairs much of the damage but not all of it. Centering the queries as well, which needs a correction term, is the next test.

Limits

This models the rounding; it is not a bit-exact copy of any GPU kernel, and it says nothing about speed. The real-model results come from one small model, four heads and one text, and don’t measure the effect on generated text. Synthetic results give medians and ranges over three seeds, not confidence intervals. Key centering used the mean over all keys at once, which a streaming kernel can’t do directly.
