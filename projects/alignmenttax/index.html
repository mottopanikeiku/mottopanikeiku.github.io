Does instruction tuning trade calibration for truthfulness? Across nine base/instruct pairs up to 32B, my answer depends on the scoring definition.

Question

A model can answer more questions correctly while becoming too confident about its mistakes. I asked whether that tradeoff survives when I use standard TruthfulQA scoring rather than a two-label derivative. The same checkpoints can look better calibrated under one definition and worse under the other.

Method

I compared nine pinned base/instruct pairs across four families: Qwen2.5 at 0.5B, 1.5B, 7B, 14B and 32B; OLMo-2 at 1B and 7B; SmolLM2-1.7B; and Mistral-7B-v0.3. The standard task uses all 817 questions and the pinned lm-evaluation-harness definitions: its exact six-example Q/A primer, space-prefixed full answers and summed token log likelihoods without length normalization. MC1 is best-answer accuracy; MC2 is probability mass on true answers, not hard accuracy. MC1 confidence is normalized across its candidate answers. Task definitions and model pins.

The separate binary derivative uses 790 questions and probabilities normalized over A/B answer labels, not full-answer likelihoods. I keep the two benchmarks separate. Both primary comparisons use shared plain prompts; native chat templates are a sensitivity that changes the checkpoint and prompt together. Standard ECE measures calibration of the chosen MC1 answer, not MC2 truth mass.

I use ten equal-frequency ECE bins and 10,000 paired question-bootstrap resamples, recomputing ECE each time. Counts require both accuracy and ECE marginal 95% intervals to exclude zero; they are not joint or multiplicity-adjusted tests. The analysis plan preceded scoring; model/tokenizer commits and methods are pinned.

Result

The tradeoff depends on the measurement. On the binary derivative, six of nine pairs gain accuracy and worsen ECE, with both intervals excluding zero. On standard MC1, four improve both accuracy and ECE, none has that resolved tradeoff, and five remain uncertain. The contrast does not prove that answer format alone causes the reversal.

Qwen2.5-14B makes the disagreement concrete. Standard MC1 rises from 40.0% to 51.4%, while its MC1 ECE falls 5.5 percentage points. The binary derivative instead raises ECE by 10.2 points. Qwen2.5-32B likewise improves standard MC1 ECE by 3.5 points while worsening binary ECE by 7.1. Exact scores and intervals.

ECE is not interchangeable with proper scores. Qwen2.5-7B’s standard ECE and Brier score improve while its negative log-likelihood worsens. Native templates change the outcome counts too: three standard pairs improve both MC1 and ECE, and five binary comparisons show a resolved tradeoff. Those are sensitivities, not the official harness default. I report all metrics rather than select one favorable story. Metric and prompt comparisons.

Limits

These are restricted-choice probabilities, not generated-answer or verbal confidence. The two benchmarks have different questions, answer sets and Brier definitions; their disagreement does not isolate a causal effect of format. Base/instruct checkpoints also differ in training stages, objectives and data, so this is not a causal effect of reinforcement learning.

Nine selected pairs are not a model-family population. Intervals resample questions, not training seeds, and are pointwise without multiplicity correction. ECE depends on binning; it is an extension to the MC1 scores, not an official MC2 calibration metric. Standard runs use H100/L4, including an OLMo-2-7B base/instruct hardware mismatch; hardware and precision variability are not in these intervals.

Inference is BF16 without quantization or CPU offload. Cache audits failed for 21 of 27 conditions, which therefore use scalar full forwards under unchanged thresholds. Those audits cover only four fixed questions per condition, not every question; no precision sweep was run. Numerical and scope limits.
