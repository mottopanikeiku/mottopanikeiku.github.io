Does instruction tuning trade calibration for truthfulness?

Question

A model can answer more questions correctly while becoming too confident about the answers it gets wrong. I asked whether instruction tuning consistently makes that trade: better truthfulness, worse confidence calibration.

Method

I compared nine matched base/instruct pairs across four families: Qwen2.5 at 0.5B, 1.5B, 7B, 14B and 32B; OLMo-2 at 1B and 7B; SmolLM2-1.7B; and Mistral-7B-v0.3. Each checkpoint was scored two ways with shared prompts. A binary derivative asks 790 TruthfulQA questions as A/B choices and normalizes the likelihoods of the two labels. Standard MC1 uses all 817 questions with the pinned lm-evaluation-harness definitions: the six-example primer and summed log-likelihoods of full answers, normalized over the candidate answers. Neither uses generated explanations or verbal confidence.

I measured accuracy and expected calibration error (ECE), using ten equal-frequency bins. The 95% intervals use 10,000 paired question resamples, recomputing ECE each time. The analysis plan was committed before scoring; model and tokenizer commits are pinned.

Result

The two scoring definitions disagree. On the binary derivative, six of nine pairs gain accuracy while ECE worsens, and two lose accuracy while ECE worsens, with both intervals excluding zero; Qwen2.5-0.5B is uncertain. On standard MC1, on the same basis, four pairs improve both accuracy and ECE, five are uncertain, and none trade calibration for accuracy.

Qwen2.5-14B’s MC1 accuracy rises from 40.0% to 51.4% and Qwen2.5-32B’s from 40.3% to 48.5%, while their standard ECE falls by 5.5 and 3.5 points. On the binary derivative, the same pairs’ ECE rises by 10.2 and 7.1 points. That is a disagreement between measurements, not proof that answer format alone causes the reversal; the next section controls two of its differences. SmolLM2-1.7B loses 15.8 points of binary accuracy and adds 32.5 points of ECE, but its MC1 change is uncertain.

The metrics also disagree: Qwen2.5-7B’s standard ECE and Brier intervals lie below zero while its negative log-likelihood interval lies above zero, so I report all of them. Native chat-template prompts are a sensitivity check, not the official harness setting: counting pairs whose intervals both exclude zero, three standard pairs improve both MC1 and ECE, and the binary derivative has five accuracy/ECE trade-offs. That comparison changes both the checkpoint and its prompt, so the figure uses only shared prompts.

Same questions, same answers

That comparison changed four things at once: the question pool, the candidate answers, the prompt and the scored continuation. Using only the saved likelihoods, I fixed the first two. I matched 779 derivative questions to standard questions by exact text, and each question’s two answers to two MC1 candidates, stripping only outer whitespace and terminal periods for the match. Each matched question is then scored twice: by its A/B-label probabilities, and by the standard-prompt likelihoods of the same two answer strings, renormalized over those two. Three questions and eight answer pairs had no unique match; they are listed with reasons.

The reversal survives. With shared prompts, the 95% interval lies above zero for label ΔECE in 8 of 9 pairs. For the same two answers scored as full strings, it lies below zero in 6 pairs, above zero in none and includes zero in 3; by point estimate, 8 are negative and 1 is positive (Qwen2.5-0.5B, +0.1 [−1.8, +2.1]). The interval for the paired difference between the two changes lies above zero in 8 of 9. In both 8-of-9 counts the ninth pair, Qwen2.5-0.5B, has an interval spanning zero and a negative point estimate. Under native chat prompts, the label intervals lie above zero in 9 of 9 pairs and the full-string intervals below zero in 8 of 9, and all 9 full-string point estimates are negative.

Descriptively, the reversal decomposes into confidence and accuracy changes. This is a decomposition, not an identified explanation, because prompt and continuation effects remain confounded. In point estimates on the matched two-answer task with shared prompts, summed full-answer likelihoods are nearly saturated before instruction tuning: every base model averages 92–94% confidence on the two answers at 28–46% accuracy. Instruct accuracy gains of up to 16 points therefore shrink the gap while confidence moves at most 3.5 points. Label probabilities start at 60–83% confidence, and instruct models raise them by 9 to 29 points while accuracy changes by −15 to +9. Qwen2.5-0.5B is the exception; its base model predicts A on 96.6% of derivative questions, and is 96.5% accurate when A is right but 3.3% when B is.

On the matched two-answer task with shared prompts, full-string confidence also ranks badly. On held-out questions, taking the most confident answers first gives a higher average error rate across coverage levels than random ordering for all 18 checkpoints; that is a point estimate, and I compute no intervals for it. For the labels, one temperature per model, fit on the other half of the questions, leaves no ECE difference whose interval excludes zero. The instruct-minus-base NLL interval lies below zero in 6 of 9 pairs and above zero in 1. The label results match two published findings: post-training hurts the calibration of lettered answers (GPT-4’s Figure 8), and a temperature largely repairs RLHF miscalibration (Kadavath et al.). But 65 of 144 temperature fits stop at the search bound of 200, including every base fit on the matched two-answer task, whose scores favor the wrong answer on average. Limits

Confidence is restricted to the offered choices, not generated answers or verbal confidence. The 817-question standard task and 790-question derivative are never pooled, and their Brier definitions differ. Base/instruct checkpoints differ in training stages and data; this is not a causal effect of reinforcement learning.

These selected checkpoints are not a model-family population. The intervals resample questions, not training seeds, and are pointwise rather than adjusted for multiple comparisons. ECE depends on binning. Standard runs used H100 and L4 GPUs, and hardware and precision variability are not in the intervals. 21 of 27 cache audits failed their tolerance, so those conditions use scalar full forward passes; the audits cover four questions per condition, and no precision sweep was run.

The matched comparison still uses different prompts (an A/B question versus the six-example primer) and different scored continuations (a label versus a full answer), and some pairs ran on different GPUs; separating those needs new inference. Its Brier score is categorical, twice the legacy binary Brier for two choices. Held-out intervals hold the split and fitted temperatures fixed, so they omit the uncertainty of refitting. MC2 has several true answers, so I report only how its truth mass moves under each model’s MC1 temperature, not an MC2 calibration.
