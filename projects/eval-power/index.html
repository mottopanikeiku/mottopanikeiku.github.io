How many benchmark items does it take to tell two language models apart?

Question

Leaderboards rank models by accuracy differences that are often a point or less. I asked three questions. How many test items does a comparison need to resolve a gap that small? If you plan that number from a small pilot, as people often do, how often does the plan reach the power it was sized for? And which planning method actually delivers the power it promises?

Simulated planning rules

First I checked five planning rules in simulations where I know the true effect. I used five paired designs, in which the two models disagree on 1% to 80% of items. Each design has a meaningful effect fixed in advance, and each study is judged by the exact McNemar test on fresh items. With a 128-item pilot, plans sized from the pilot’s observed gap detected the effect 15–68% of the time instead of 80%. Plans that fixed the effect and used the pilot’s variance reached 77–78% when at least a fifth of items were discordant. They reached 72% at 5% discordance, 59% at 1% and 47% for a 10-item design. Plans that replaced that variance with a 95% upper bound on the discordance rate detected the effect 84–95% of the time in every design. They needed 1.2–2.4 times the median budget.

A Gaussian budget is not exact even with the true variance. For budgets in the hundreds or thousands, the exact test needs 2.5–8% more items than the formula. For a 10-item design, the formula’s budget has 48% power, and the exact test needs 18 items. With a 32-item pilot and 1% discordance, every discordance-bound plan for a half-point comparison needs more than my 20,000-item cap. These are simulations of independent items, not measurements of models. Data: nested.csv; methods.

Leaderboard data

The data are per-question right and wrong answers from 395 models on five benchmarks of the 2024 Open LLM Leaderboard, about 28,000 items in all. Both models in a comparison answer the same questions, so the comparison can be paired. Pairing removes the difficulty that the two models share. To test pilot planning, I split the items into a small pilot and a held-out pool. I sized a full study from the gap seen in the pilot, then checked how often that study detected the difference on held-out items.

Leaderboard gaps

Most leaderboard gaps are unresolved. Even without a correction for multiple comparisons, only 1 to 12 of the 394 neighboring pairs in each benchmark’s ranking differ significantly. Failing to reject a gap does not show that it is zero, and these neighbors were chosen by their observed ranks. To resolve a hypothetical one-point gap at 80% power, a paired comparison would need roughly 6,000 to 17,000 items per model, and an unpaired one 22,000 to 38,000. Most of these benchmarks have far fewer items than that. I did not test one-point gaps.

Planning from a pilot’s gap did not deliver the power it was sized for. Studies sized from a 128-item pilot to have 80% power detected the difference only 49% to 79% of the time, depending on the benchmark. A larger pilot did not reliably help. The gap that a small pilot sees is itself noisy, so plans built on it overshoot and undershoot by wide margins. This is a known problem in statistics. A safer approach is to fix the smallest difference that matters before collecting any data, and the simulation above measures how well that works.

Prospective study

Next I generated 13,830 primary-study answers from six small models: Qwen2.5 at 1.5B, 3B and 7B; SmolLM2-1.7B; Phi-3.5-mini; and Mistral-7B-v0.3. Each model answered the same 64 pilot questions per benchmark, once greedily and five times at temperature 0.7. I committed the sample-size plans before collecting answers on fresh items. The protocol pins the models and decoding, and the study report records the answer counts.

Twenty-three of 27 planned comparisons detected a difference at the prespecified, uncorrected 5% level: 13/14 on GSM8K and 10/13 on guided direct-choice ARC-Challenge. I also applied a post hoc Holm adjustment. Over all 30 planned pairs, with the three unrun plans counted as p = 1, 17 detections remain. If only the 27 executed tests form the family, 18 remain, and with one family per benchmark, 19 remain. Each feasible pair used its planned 16–264 fresh questions and a paired t-test on five-answer item means. Most realized gaps were large. The median was 16.9 points, one gap was within a point and 13 were above 20 points, and nine comparisons used the 16-item minimum. So this confirms plans for large separations, not budgets for one-point gaps. The plans were also sized from each pilot’s observed gap, which is the rule the simulation warns against for close models. This is an observed fraction, not proof of 80% power, because effects differ and pairs share models and answers. Three plans needed more than the declared 512-question pool and were not run. I did not cut them down to fit the pool and then call them 80%-power tests. Data: sensitivity audit.

Decoding adds a much larger share of the variance on GSM8K. With five stochastic answers per question, its median estimated share is 33.2%, against 4.6% on direct-choice ARC. I estimate the variance within questions and subtract its contribution from the observed variance of paired item means. I keep the unclipped estimate and apply a zero floor only for planning. These are estimates from a finite pilot, not exactly identified population components.

The plans were fixed after a pilot, not in a protocol registered before any pilot. Before the confirmation run, I fixed numeric grading and a bug that reused random seeds, collected the primary pilots again, and switched all six ARC models to guided Answer: <label> generation. Direct choice is not the usual ARC option-likelihood evaluation. The revision comparison records those changes.

Historical votes

I also looked at a separate historical Arena release, where 54,985 retained votes cover 55 models. Only four of 54 adjacent model contrasts exclude zero under either pointwise Wald or bootstrap 95% intervals. The other 50 are unresolved; that does not show they are ties. These are historical votes, not newly collected preferences or a current leaderboard. The adjacent intervals are not simultaneous and not adjusted for rank selection. Data: Arena summary.

Limits

The planning simulation assumes independent binary items and the exact McNemar test. It does not check the repeated-decoding t-test, clustered items, prompt variation or adaptive stopping. Its conservative bound plans for the power of a single test, not familywise power.

The earlier leaderboard analysis uses older open models, many of them fine-tunes or merges, with each answer recorded once. Its resampling of held-out answers is separate from the new prospective collection. Neither study is a benchmark of current frontier models.

The prospective study uses public benchmarks and small instruction-tuned models, so contamination is possible. A finite pilot can misestimate both effects and variance. The comparisons overlap. The protocol did not prespecify a multiplicity correction, so the Holm counts are post hoc. Five stochastic draws are not greedy decoding. Fewer questions do not mean fewer generated answers or lower compute cost. The Arena model assumes IID votes and transitive preference strength. The retained aggregates cannot recover user or prompt clustering, because that information is not available.
