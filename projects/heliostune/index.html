HeliosTune

I tested whether the kernel bank was the limit. Ten added Hopper-aware actions beat torch.matmul on 9 of 96 H100 workloads, where the old 36 actions won on none. The new bank is faster overall, but torch still wins most workloads.

Question

My earlier audit found no action faster than torch.matmul in the measured bank. Could a wider action set recover wins without changing the search policy? I added larger tiles, deeper pipelines, persistent TMA and tensor-core split-K for small-row decode shapes.

Method

I fixed the action set and analysis plan before collection, then remeasured 96 named workloads covering 84 unique matrix shapes on one H100. Six new configurations apply at every row count; four split-K configurations apply only at M = 1, 7, 31. I compare the ten new actions with the old 36, selecting the lowest valid median separately on bank 1 and scoring those fixed winners against same-session torch on bank 2. The expanded union also chooses old versus new on bank 1, never on the scoring bank.

The allocating FP16 calls include split-K workspace, both kernel launches and the final reduction. I used 25 ms warmup and 100 ms timing. The ratio is candidate latency / torch latency, so below one favors Triton; geometric means weight named workloads equally. This is new measurement, not the earlier stored-timing audit. Protocol and per-workload records.

The original tuner uses nearby-shape retrieval and Bayesian linear Thompson sampling. I did not rerun its transfer policies on the expanded bank. The new split-K implementation computes FP32 partial tiles, then reduces and converts once to FP16; the persistent path builds on Triton’s pinned tutorial.

Result

The action set was a limit. The new bank wins on 9/96 workloads, loses on 86 and exactly ties one; the remeasured old bank wins on none. Its geometric-mean latency / torch ratio falls from 1.608× to 1.174×. New actions are faster than old on 95/96 workloads, but do not close the gap overall. These are descriptive median comparisons, not significance tests.

Six wins use split-K at M = 1 or 7; three use larger ordinary tiles; none uses persistent TMA. The largest is Qwen2.5-7B’s FFN down-projection at (M,N,K) = (1,3584,18944): 63.456 µs versus 67.184 µs, 5.55% lower latency. Five of the nine winning margins are under 1%. Adding decode split-K recovered a few shapes, not a general advantage over torch. All nine nominal wins.

Earlier audit and transfer

The earlier audit reanalyzed old stored timings, rather than the new collection above. Torch.matmul had lower stored median latency than the independently selected reference on all 96 workloads; the geometric-mean reference / torch ratio was 1.610016×. Even choosing each workload’s fastest of the 36 configurations on the scoring bank could not beat torch. That was an optimistic in-sample diagnostic, but it showed that better search could not erase the gap within that fixed timing matrix.

The separate GPU overview counts lower torch medians on 31/96 L4, 31/96 A10, 94/96 T4 and 96/96 H100 workloads. L4 and A10 are mixed, not uniformly dominated. These are within-GPU summaries from separate acquisition stages, not a pooled four-GPU policy result.

The historical transfer comparison is smaller: cold-start Thompson sampling scored 0.958434 against Parhelion’s 0.950259, averaged over budgets 1–8. Here score means reference_latency / selected_latency, so higher is better. The original aggregation averages model-family geometric means over policy seeds, then averages the eight budgets; it differs from the audit’s single workload geometric mean.

Both selected transfer strengths were zero. Parhelion still uses a retrieval anchor and source features: this does not show retrieval is useless. Retrieval gave it a better first probe, but cold-start search overtook it on the second.

Limits

This is one H100 collection, not device replication. Five wins are under 1%; timing quantiles describe spread, not confidence intervals. Individual timing samples are not retained, and repeated matrix shapes across models are not independent devices. The new bank’s nominal wins do not establish a reliable advantage on every repeat.

Warmed medians omit compilation, policy computation, acquisition and serving overhead. The new collection records torch’s reduced-FP16 reduction setting as enabled. Correctness uses FP16 operands with an FP32-output reference and TF32 disabled, at atol=rtol=0.01; passing this check does not prove identical arithmetic. The older audit did not retain torch’s within-run spreads or fully record its timed accumulation settings; its limitations remain separate.

The expanded bank is still small and does not identify a hardware optimum or a causal bottleneck. I did not rerun transfer policies on it. The separate H200 engineering run uses a different protocol and is not pooled here. Limits and history.
