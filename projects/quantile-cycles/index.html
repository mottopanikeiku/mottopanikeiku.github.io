Quantile cycles

I found problems where quantile-based distributional RL does not converge for control, and I checked the smallest case in Lean. In a sampled test, the Huber failure I specified in advance did not appear.

Question

Quantile-based distributional RL describes each action’s return distribution with a few quantiles and picks the action whose quantiles have the highest mean. When the policy is fixed, hard quantile-projected Bellman updates converge. I wanted to know whether they also converge for control, where the greedy action can change. I also wanted to know whether a failure would still show up when the updates are learned from samples with a Huber loss.

Method

I built problems with one state and two actions and checked their exact Bellman backups in rational arithmetic. My paper proof covers a matching problem for every quantile count K ≥ 2. I also formalized the smallest rational instance in Lean.

Then I ran a sampled test. I compared hard projection, pinball and Huber updates with a scalar learner that saw the same reward draws in each seed. The test used 32 seeds, 100,000 updates and batches of 32 target pairs per action. Before the run, I fixed the methods, the endpoint and the rule for deciding whether to go on to replay and network experiments.

Cycles in the exact operator

The exact operator does not converge. For every K ≥ 2, I construct a problem with one state and two actions that has no fixed point. Every finite real starting table approaches a strict two-cycle, up to phase. On the cycle, the greedy choice alternates between the unique optimal action and a worse one. No greedy tie is needed.

In the smallest case there are two quantiles and the discount is 1/4. Action A always pays 91/128. Action B pays 0, 1/2 or 1 with probabilities 3/16, 1/2 and 5/16. So A has a true mean-reward advantage of 19/128, and always choosing B loses 19/96 in discounted value. But the projected means favor A by 3/128 in one phase and B by 3/128 in the next (exact certificates, phase diagram).

I also built larger problems from a delay chain, which keeps the discount fixed at 0.9. For these I only show local attraction: starts near the cycle approach it. I do not claim that all starts approach one common phase. The table gives their sizes.

Lean check

Lean’s kernel checks the specific rational cycle with K=2 and discount 1/4. The check covers the actual target laws, all eight generalized-inverse quantiles, the strict greedy choices and both exact backups. The proof has no sorry. It uses only Lean’s standard axioms: propext, Classical.choice and Quot.sound (axiom listings, build record). I have not formalized the general real-valued theorem.

Sampled test

Before the sampled test, I specified what would count as a material Huber failure. It did not appear. None of the four required comparisons met my rule for continuing, so I did not run replay or network training. With K=32 and constant steps, Huber’s normalized regret was 0.0043 and the scalar learner’s was 0.0783. The paired difference was −0.0740, with a 95% interval of [−0.0766, −0.0713]. So in this comparison Huber did better than the scalar learner.

Limits

I have not checked the general theorem in Lean, and nobody else has peer-reviewed it. The Lean proof of the finite cycle does not rule out other fixed points. Attraction, the theorem for arbitrary K and the delay construction are still paper proofs. Separately, my exact verifier checks 18 one-state instances, five delay instances and 18 zero-start trajectories (verification record).

The sampled study is tabular and synthetic. It draws from a generative sampler and has no exploration, replay or neural network. Each K uses a different problem from the family. Hard projection, pinball and Huber are different operators. Finite runs cannot prove convergence or rule out rare failing seeds. A policy that switches noisily between actions does not prove an exact cycle. The counterexample for the hard operator is not evidence that QR-DQN training fails.
