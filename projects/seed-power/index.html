How many independent training seeds does a reinforcement learning comparison need? I test which ways of planning that number from a small pilot actually reach the power they are sized for.

Question

A small pilot gives a rough gap and variance for two training setups, and turning them into a seed count is easy. The question is whether that count delivers. I first ran large fresh-seed experiments with the usual rule, which plans from the gap the pilot observed. They detected far fewer differences than the 80% target, but they could not say why, because I never knew the true gap. So I compared five planning rules in simulations where I do know it, and checked the best one on the committed PPO scores.

Simulated planning rules

I simulated four kinds of seed scores: Gaussian, Gaussian with a doubled treatment SD, scores bounded by a ceiling that 10% of seeds hit, and a failure/success mixture with 26% failures. Each has a true null and a meaningful effect of half the control SD, fixed in advance. A simulated study runs a pilot of 8, 16, 32 or 64 seeds per arm and plans a count for 80% power. It then runs that many fresh seeds per arm and applies Welch’s test. Plans above 512 seeds per arm are not run, as in my fresh-seed experiments. For Gaussian scores I computed power exactly by quadrature, and the simulation matched it within 2.73 Monte Carlo standard errors.

Planning from the pilot’s observed gap gave 36.3–80.5% power when the true effect equalled the meaningful one. It also put 25.0–68.0% of null pilots above 512 seeds per arm. Fixing the effect in advance and plugging in the pilot variance did much better, but with 8-seed pilots it reached only 75.5–77.0%. Julious and Owen’s noncentral-t rule adds an allowance for how uncertain the pilot variance is. It kept power at 79.3–83.3% in every population and at every pilot size. On average, plain plug-in used 0.95 times as many seeds as this rule. Browne’s 80% upper confidence limit on the variance gave more power but used 1.16 times as many. With no true difference, the fixed-effect rules rejected 4.7–5.4%, and 5.3–6.8% when the two arms had equal means but different shapes.

I then checked the rule on the committed PPO roots by resampling them, which is not new training. I split each pool of roots into disjoint halves and drew each pilot and confirmation without replacement, so no root appears twice in one simulated study. When I shifted one base arm by half its SD, the rule gave 80.5–82.6% power. It rejected 4.5–5.3% of base-versus-base nulls. The repository’s planner now applies this rule when given the pilot size.

Fresh-seed experiments

I used established power calculations and affine ARS/CEM policy search adapted from coldstart. I fixed six different-variant comparisons per task on CartPole and Acrobot, changing iteration counts or comparing the optimizers. Each repeat used disjoint eight-seed pilots per arm to estimate the gap and variance, then plan a study targeting 80% power. A policy score averages sixteen evaluation episodes; those episodes are not extra training seeds. The README describes the design and its assumptions.

I committed and pushed the complete pilot plan before confirmation. I then trained 11,440 fresh seeds, separate from 2,688 pilot seeds, and tested the resulting comparisons with Welch’s test. Every executable plan received its exact requested seed count (counts).

I then repeated the design with neural PPO, using two 32-unit hidden layers per actor and critic, vectorized across independent seeds on one L4. Base PPO is compared with faster and slower learning rates, two rather than four update epochs, and no entropy bonus, 50 repetitions per pair. Every seed trains exactly 32,768 CartPole or 65,536 Acrobot environment steps. I pushed the complete count plan before confirmation, then trained 65,744 fresh seeds, separate from 8,000 pilot seeds (neural protocol).

Fresh-seed results

With affine policy search, plans sized for 80% modeled power from each pilot’s observed gap detected a difference in 67 of 119 executable different-variant comparisons: 56.3%, with a descriptive Wilson 95% interval of 47.3–64.9%. CartPole detected differences in 30/60 comparisons (50.0%); Acrobot in 37/59 (62.7%). This is detection frequency for a fixed set of short-budget comparisons, not power at a known effect.

Of 144 attempted different-variant plans, 25 needed more than the predeclared limit of 256 seeds per arm and were not run. I did not cap their counts and still call them 80% plans. Seven detections reversed the pilot’s direction. Separate same-variant controls rejected in 1/17 tests; that small sample cannot establish exact type-I calibration (outcomes).

With neural PPO, the plans detected a difference in 139 of 307 executable comparisons: 45.3%, with a Wilson 95% interval of 39.8–50.9%. CartPole detected 109/175 (62.3%); Acrobot only 30/132 (22.7%).

Of 400 attempted neural plans, 93 needed more than the limit of 512 seeds per arm; I kept their requested counts rather than truncate them. Twenty detections reversed the pilot’s direction. Same-variant controls rejected in 7/74 tests (9.5%; 4.7–18.3%), so I do not claim exact 5% calibration. The two experiments differ in optimizer, budget, horizon and comparison mix, so the gap between 56.3% and 45.3% is not a causal effect of neural policies.

The simulation shows one possible mechanism for this shortfall, not its measured cause: even with the truth known, observed-gap plans reached only 36.3–80.5% power in four stylized populations. In the resampling check, three PPO variant pairs (CartPole no_entropy, Acrobot lr_slow and Acrobot no_entropy against base) have finite-population gaps that, under the noncentral-t planning model, need far more than 512 seeds per arm.

Limits

These are two classic-control tasks, not a general deep-RL benchmark. The achieved rates are conditional on plans that fit the seed limit, and pooling different comparisons makes the overall intervals descriptive. In the affine study, twelve repeats per comparison leave wide intervals, and fixed iteration budgets are not equal environment-step budgets.

In the fresh-seed experiments, the planner treated noisy pilot gaps and variances as population values. Its equal-variance Gaussian calculation is pooled-t power; using it for Welch’s test is an approximation. Bounded, skewed RL returns need not fit that model, and some different-variant comparisons may have no true mean difference. Those results do not show that all missed detections came from pilot noise, or estimate power for RL in general (interpretation and limits).

The simulation uses four stylized score shapes and one effect size, so other shapes or much smaller effects may behave differently. Every fixed-effect rule needs an effect chosen in advance; if the true effect is only half of it, simulated power falls to 28.4–41.3%. The resampling check reuses the committed roots, depends on the fixed evaluation resets and is not a prospective test of the rule on newly trained seeds. I have not yet explained the CartPole same-variant rejections (5/36).
