Verge Lab

I trained models on aspect-selected preference pairs. Pareto selection showed no clear advantage over a simple score-gap rule: the reward difference was −0.112, with a 95% interval crossing zero.

Question

Does refusing aspect tradeoffs select better training pairs than choosing a large overall-score gap? I keep a pair only when one response is at least as good on every chosen aspect and better on at least one. The test is whether training on those selected pairs improves a held-out reward score, not just whether their labels agree with people.

Method

I trained nine Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct DPO adapters: three matched seeds for each of Pareto selection, helpfulness-score-gap selection and a human-preference reference. Every selector supplied 1,024 pairs from the same eligible HelpSteer2 training pool. My Pareto rule uses correctness and coherence; helpfulness supplies the gap baseline, while complexity and verbosity are not maximized. The selectors choose different pairs, so this tests their complete selection rules rather than isolating label direction. I kept training settings unchanged across conditions. Training setup.

I evaluated generated answers on 192 held-out HelpSteer2 prompts with the pinned OpenAssistant DeBERTa reward model. None of these prompt hashes appears anywhere in the HelpSteer2 training pool. There is one starting-model response per prompt, shared across conditions. The scorer is independent of the selector’s ratings, but it is a reward proxy, not a human judge. I use 10,000 crossed paired bootstrap draws over prompt IDs and matched training seed IDs; the 576 condition records are not independent prompts. Design and analysis.

Result

The trained-model result is inconclusive. Pareto minus score-gap reward is −0.112 raw logits, with a crossed prompt/seed 95% interval of [−0.257,+0.032]. Gap selection has the higher point estimate, but this does not establish its superiority or practical equivalence. The three matched-seed mean differences are −0.027, −0.118 and −0.190.

Reward-model wins against the starting model were 49.8% for Pareto, 54.9% for score gap and 50.0% for human labels. Each condition has 576 comparisons across the same 192 prompts and three seeds; ties stay in the denominator. This is not a human win rate. Condition counts and rewards.

Earlier human-agreement check

I found no clear advantage on HelpSteer2 validation. Both selectors kept 310 pairs. Pareto agreed with 281/286 strict human preferences (98.25%); the matched overall-score-gap baseline agreed with 277/282 (98.23%). Both contradicted 5 human preferences.

Equal yield does not mean identical pairs or equal numbers of strict human labels. Pareto’s selected pairs include 24 human ties, leaving 286 strict preferences; the baseline’s include 28, leaving 282. I do not count ties as agreement. The baseline also varies with label-blind tie-breaking, so the tiny headline difference does not establish superiority.

Earlier, I tested the first 200 TruthfulQA prompts in UltraFeedback, using GPT-4 aspect ratings. I kept 820 of 1,200 pairs. Of the kept pairs with a strict separate overall ranking, 132/706 (18.7%) went the other way. This measures inconsistency between LLM ratings, not human preference errors.

Limits

One small model, three training seeds and 1,024 pairs per selector limit generalization. The independent scorer is an older reward proxy, not a direct measure of human preference. Its declared training sources predate HelpSteer2, but older-source overlap, shared pretraining and unknown Qwen instruction data remain possible. The interval crossing zero is inconclusive; it does not prove no benefit or equivalence.

Generation stops at 192 new tokens, and every condition’s median reaches that limit. The reward scorer truncates 117/1,920 inputs to 512 tokens. Pareto responses are about 4.14 tokens longer than gap responses; length may affect reward, and I report it without a post-hoc adjustment. Training truncation can also remove prompt context. Training and scorer limits.

The earlier human ratings are rounded, filtered annotations, not objective truth; separate preference collection does not guarantee independent annotators. The UltraFeedback prefix is not a random sample and uses unverified GPT-4 ratings. The browser tool still uses authored illustrative examples, not these training or evaluation rows.
