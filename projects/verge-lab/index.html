Verge Lab

I choose preference pairs from aspect scores and abstain when the aspects trade off.

Question

Does refusing aspect tradeoffs select better preference pairs than choosing pairs with a large overall-score gap, and does DPO on those pairs produce a better model? I keep a pair only when one response is at least as good on every chosen aspect and better on at least one, rather than averaging away disagreement.

Method

I joined HelpSteer2’s human aspect ratings to its separately collected pairwise preferences by exact prompt and response hashes. My primary Pareto rule compares correctness and coherence. I hold out overall helpfulness for the baseline, which selects the largest absolute helpfulness gaps and predicts their direction. I exclude helpfulness ties from both selectors, match their selected pair counts, and evaluate against the separate human preferences. Equal-gap tie-breaking does not use preference labels.

I take the normalized ratings at face value, with no uncertainty penalty. Complexity and verbosity describe style, so I do not maximize them in the primary rule.

To test training benefit, I built three fixed sets of 1,024 pairs from the same eligible pool: a hash-ordered sample of Pareto pairs, the largest absolute helpfulness gaps, and a hash-ordered human-preference reference. I trained nine Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct LoRA adapters with identical TRL DPO settings, three seeds per set, and scored their answers to 192 held-out HelpSteer2 prompts with a pinned OpenAssistant DeBERTa reward model. I committed the plan and exact datasets before training.

Result

DPO on Pareto pairs did not beat the helpfulness-gap baseline. Pareto minus gap reward was −0.112 logits, with a crossed prompt/seed 95% interval of −0.257 to +0.032. The gap selector had the higher point estimate in all three seeds, but my precommitted decision is inconclusive, not superiority or equivalence.

Pareto answers were about 4.1 tokens longer than gap answers, and every condition’s median length reached the 192-token generation cap, so longer-answer behavior is not evaluated.

Before training, I compared the selectors against HelpSteer2’s separate human preferences on validation and also found no clear advantage. Both selectors kept 310 pairs. Pareto agreed with 281/286 strict human preferences (98.25%); the matched overall-score-gap baseline agreed with 277/282 (98.23%). Both contradicted 5 human preferences.

Equal yield does not mean identical pairs or equal numbers of strict human labels. Pareto’s selected pairs include 24 human ties, leaving 286 strict preferences; the baseline’s include 28, leaving 282. I do not count ties as agreement. The baseline also varies with label-blind tie-breaking, so the tiny headline difference does not establish superiority.

Earlier, I tested the first 200 TruthfulQA prompts in UltraFeedback, using GPT-4 aspect ratings. I kept 820 of 1,200 pairs. Of the kept pairs with a strict separate overall ranking, 132/706 (18.7%) went the other way. This measures inconsistency between LLM ratings, not human preference errors.

Limits

I tested small HelpSteer2 samples. Their human ratings are rounded, filtered annotations, not objective truth; separately collected preferences do not guarantee independent annotators. The DPO comparison uses an older reward model as a proxy, not a human judge; its declared training sources predate HelpSteer2, but shared pretraining is not ruled out. Three seeds and 1,024 pairs per set limit generalization, and the three sets select different pairs, so this tests complete selectors rather than label direction alone. The earlier UltraFeedback sample is a deterministic prefix, not a random sample, and uses unverified GPT-4 ratings rather than human labels. The browser review tool uses authored illustrative examples, not these benchmark rows.
